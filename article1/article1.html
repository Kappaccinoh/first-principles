1. The Vertical Commute

My campus dormitory, Saga College, is a wonderfully made 23-story building overlooking the UTown Green. While the architecture is impressive, the lifts have historically been a major point of contention within our community. Students living on the highest levels are often compensated with great views and cooler weather, but those perks are frequently overshadowed by the frustration of long waiting times. During peak periods, it is not uncommon to wait up to five minutes for a lift; a ridiculous delay when you are rushing for a class.

To combat these unequal wait times, the community developed an implicit social contract: the non-consecutive floor policy. The idea is simple: if you live on the 5th floor and the 4th or 6th floor has already been pressed, you take the initiative to walk one flight of stairs rather than forcing the lift to stop again. While this arrangement benefits the community’s overall efficiency, it remains a point of friction. Every year, seniors find themselves lecturing the incoming batch on lift etiquette, trying to bridge the gap between individual convenience and collective speed.

This problem isn’t just a social one; it invites a deeper analysis into elevator algorithms. How do we efficiently program a lift to ensure all students are serviced fairly without the Stop-and-Go cycles that lead to these exceedingly long waits? This article is an exploration of lift algorithm design—from simple simulations to the current frontier of the field—to see if we can close the gap between academic theory and the daily reality of community living.

2. Defining the Baseline: How a Lift Thinks

At its core, the problem at hand is classified as a stochastic optimisation problem. It is stochastic because passenger arrivals are random and unpredictable, and it is an optimisation problem because we are interested in finding the best way to utilise a limited resource—in this case, our two lift cars—to service a 23-story building.

To evaluate any algorithm, we first need a way to measure the cost of a lift’s journey.

2.1 The Math of the Lift Cycle

In a simplified model, the efficiency of a lift is described by two factors: how far it travels and how many times it stops. We can model the total time of a single journey (\(T\)) using a linear equation: \[T = (D \cdot t_f) + (S \cdot t_s)\]

Where:

  • \(D\): The total distance (number of floors) the lift travels.
  • \(t_f\): The constant time taken to pass a single floor without stopping.
  • \(S\): The number of stops made during the journey.
  • \(t_s\): The Stop Penalty—the fixed time lost for doors opening, passenger transfer, and doors closing.

While \(D\), \(t_s\), and \(t_f\) are physical constants, \(S\) is a discrete random variable dependent on passenger demand. Consequently, the total journey time \(T\) becomes a function of the expected number of stops \(E[S]\).

\[E[T] = (D \cdot t_f) + t_s \cdot E[S]\] \[Var(T) = t^2_s \cdot Var(S)\]

The assumption that \(t_s \gg t_f\) underscores the sensitivity of the model; small fluctuations in \(S\) result in disproportionately large swings in the obtained value of \(T\). The non consecutive floor policy is a community-driven attempt to minimise \(Var(T)\) to stabilise \(E[T]\).

2.2 The SCAN Algorithm

The SCAN algorithm, often known simply as the Elevator Algorithm, is a classic piece of logic that dates back to the 1980s (Wikipedia contributors 2024). It was designed for an era where computer memory was expensive, so the instructions had to be as simple as possible.

The defining characteristic of SCAN is its naive travel direction. We define the lift’s position at any time \(t\) as \(f(t) \in \{1, 2, \dots, 23\}\). The direction of travel \(d(t)\) is a binary state that only switches when the lift hits the terminal floors:

\[d(t+1) = \begin{cases} 1 (\text{Up}) & \text{if } f(t) = 1 \\ -1 (\text{Down}) & \text{if } f(t) = 23 \end{cases}\]

Because the lift is forced to travel the full 23 stories (\(1 \to 23 \to 1\)) regardless of the density of requests, the distance variable \(D\) in our earlier equation is essentially locked in as a constant (\(2 \cdot D_{max}\)). This mathematical rigidity means that the total cycle time \(T\) becomes almost entirely dependent on the realization of the random variable \(S\):

\[T_{cycle} = (2 \cdot D_{max} \cdot t_f) + (S \cdot t_s)\]
